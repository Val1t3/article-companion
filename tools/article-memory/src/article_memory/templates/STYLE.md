# Style guide

Editorial rules for this article, which is published as an X Article (long-form post on X). The writer applies them; the editor and the style-reviewer judge the draft against them. Rules are grouped by section so reviews can cite them (e.g. "Hook, rule 2").

## Accuracy

1. Every factual statement (number, date, event, name, study result, quote, attribution) comes from `research/facts.json` and says no more than the fact's `quote` supports. Numbers are copied exactly, with their unit and scope.
2. A factual statement links inline to the URL of its source.
3. When a needed fact is missing, write around it or state the limit honestly. Never fill the gap from memory.
4. Opinions are allowed, and encouraged, but they are presented as the author's view, not as facts.

## Title

1. The title contains a number, a provocative claim, or an unresolved tension. A vague title that could describe any article on the topic is forbidden.
2. Proven formulas: "[Number] [Topic] That [Unexpected Qualifier]", "Why [Topic] Is More [Adjective] Than You Think", "[Number] Most Important [Topic] Explained in Just [Time]".
3. The title opens a gap the reader wants to close, and honestly reflects the angle the article develops. No empty clickbait.

## Structure

1. Three-part arc: hook, body sections, conclusion. At least 3 `##` sections in total.
2. A reader grasps the whole article in under 20 seconds by scanning the headings alone: each heading carries a standalone idea, not a vague label.
3. One core idea per section. About 150-250 words per body section, never more than 400.
4. Sections build toward a cumulative payoff; they do not just list facts. No section repeats another.
5. Length matches the brief. Without a length in the brief, aim for about 1200 words (a 5-8 minute read).

## Hook (first section)

1. The first 3-5 sentences do NOT define, summarize, or state a general fact about the topic. They open a cognitive or emotional gap.
2. Open with one of: a personal-feeling scene with tension, an unexpected admission, a relatable frustration, or a provocative contrast (what most people believe, then the challenge). Any anecdote must be framed as illustrative, never presented as a real reported event unless it is a fact from the research.
3. Short, punchy sentences, and a first line that stands alone on a phone screen (under about 15 words). Vary the rhythm: not one sentence per line throughout. Do not start with a rhetorical question; lead with a statement or a scene.
4. End the hook with a one-sentence promise of what the article delivers. No bullet list in the hook.

## Tone and voice

1. First person ("I", "you", "we"), active voice. Sound like explaining to a smart friend.
2. Defend a clear, specific point of view, backed by the facts. Neutral, detached reporting underperforms.
3. Be confident: definitive statements, no hedges like "seems" or "perhaps", but strong claims always rest on concrete evidence.
4. Acknowledge the reader's likely frustration before making the argument; use inclusive language that validates their experience.
5. Plain language: short words beat long words. Explain any technical term immediately.

## Explanations and analogies

1. Analogy first: when introducing a complex or abstract concept, lead with an everyday analogy BEFORE the technical explanation.
2. The analogy comes from daily life (objects, routines, common jobs), maps cleanly to the concept in at most 3 sentences, then connects back explicitly ("X works the same way.").
3. At least one concrete example per concept, distinct from the analogy (real tools, real scenarios, real numbers from the facts), placed after the analogy.
4. Depth for a curious non-expert: what it is (one line), why it matters (one line), how it works (analogy + example).
5. Rhetorical questions may pre-empt confusion ("But why does this matter?") and are answered immediately; never left hanging.

## Flow

1. Each section opens with a micro-hook specific to its topic and closes with a bridge that lands the takeaway or propels the reader to the next section.
2. At most 3 lines per paragraph (1-2 for key statements). One idea per paragraph. No wall of text.
3. Vary sentence length: short sentences (<20 words) for facts and emphasis, longer ones only for nuance. Fragments are allowed for effect.
4. Spoken transitions guide the reader and carry logic ("But there's a catch.", "So ...", "Instead of ..."). No staged run-ups ("Here's the thing.", "Now here's the important part:", "Let that sink in.").
5. No meta-commentary about the article itself, no hollow recap sentences.

## Formatting

1. Natural, human-sounding English. No AI-tell phrases or filler: never "delve into", "it's important to note", "in today's world", "in conclusion", "navigating the landscape", "unlock", "leverage" (as filler), "game-changer".
2. **Bold** only for key terms or pivotal phrases, 2-3 times per section at most; never a full sentence.
3. Prefer flowing prose. Lists only for genuinely parallel items, never for continuous reasoning, never two lists back to back.
4. Inline code for technical names, file names and commands (e.g. `CLAUDE.md`).
5. No em dashes or en dashes as punctuation: use periods, commas, colons or parentheses. Use whitespace for rhythm.
6. The whole article (title, headings, body, CTA) is written in English, even when the brief or the sources are in another language. Quotes and names of things from non-English sources are translated into English (proper names stay as they are); no sentence or passage is left in another language.

## Conclusion and CTA (last section)

1. Does NOT summarize or recap the article. Never begins with "In conclusion" or "To summarize".
2. Elevates the argument: a broader implication, a new lens on the whole piece, or a direct challenge to act or reflect.
3. The final sentence of the conclusion is memorable: it is what the reader carries away. The conclusion, not the CTA, is the emotional peak.
4. Ends with a short, X-native call-to-action (under 3 sentences, the only CTA in the article): thank the reader in plain words, then one ask: a genuine question inviting replies, a follow with a brief honest reason, or a bookmark if the article is a how-to. No claps, no "like and repost".
